# The Visitor: research (step 1)

The user's brief (DECISIONS 2026-10-28, evening): a first-person horror game set in Japan, in one apartment. An old
friend and first crush comes to visit, and things start to go wrong. Step 1 was "find real life scenarios or stories
for inspiration", and this is that step. Each entry says what happened, why it fits, and what we could take from it.
The facts come from the sources at the end; where sources disagree, the entry says so. Real cases give us shapes only,
never names or details (§9). The abstract built on this is `02_abstract.md`.

## 1. Missing people in Japan

### 1.1 The numbers
National Police Agency figures: 90,144 missing-person reports in 2023, up from 84,910 in 2022. Dementia accounts for
about a fifth (19,039 in 2023, a record; 18,121 in 2024, of whom 491 were found dead). Most of those people are found
within a week.
- **Why it fits:** in Japan a missing poster on a station board or the building's notice board is everyday; nobody
  looks twice. That lets us put one in the corridor in the first minute without it reading as a horror cliché.
- **What we take:** the corridor's notice board (a faded poster from twelve years ago, a new one this week), the TV
  news, the officer from the local police box (kōban) going door to door.

### 1.2 Jōhatsu: people who leave on purpose
The "evaporated" are people who walk out of their lives on purpose, from debt, shame, abuse or pressure. Some are
helped by *yonige-ya* ("night-flight" movers) who move them out overnight, for ¥50,000 to ¥300,000 by one account.
Most of the coverage is journalism (Léna Mauger and Stéphane Remael's *The Vanished* is the best known), and its
numbers vary.
- **Why it fits:** someone can be reported missing because they chose to vanish, phone left behind, and be alive and
  close by.
- **What we take:** the visitor herself is "missing". She left Tokyo on the 27th with a note and without her phone, and
  her family reported her. The player sees her face on the news and thinks something has happened to a woman who looks
  like her, or that she's that woman's ghost. She's simply the woman on the news.

### 1.3 Seven years, then a funeral: the declaration of disappearance
Civil Code art. 30: when a person's whereabouts and survival have been unknown for seven years, the family court can,
at a family member's request, declare them missing (*shissō senkoku*). Art. 31: they are then treated as dead from the
end of those seven years (or one year after a shipwreck, a war or another peril). Inheritance opens and a marriage
ends.
- **Why it fits:** a family can bury a daughter without a body, years after she vanished, because they've decided to
  stop waiting. Someone in the family may not have.
- **What we take:** this spring Natsumi Kikuchi's parents had her declared dead, twelve years after she vanished, and
  held a funeral with an empty coffin. Her younger sister didn't accept it.

### 1.4 The mourning postcard (*mochū hagaki*)
A household that lost someone during the year sends no New Year cards. Instead, in November or early December, it
sends a mourning postcard so that nobody sends it festive greetings. Many people learn of a death this way.
- **Why it fits:** it's a real, everyday Japanese object that almost no Western player knows, and it arrives in the
  very weeks the story happens.
- **What we take:** in the unopened mail, word from the Kikuchis that Natsumi was declared dead. (The game moved to
  August, §5b, so it's a black-bordered letter with an invitation to her first Obon rather than a New Year card.) The
  player can read it while tidying, minutes before Natsumi knocks.

## 2. Homes that hide things

### 2.1 The missing centenarians (2010) and the 8050 problem
In July 2010, officials visiting Sogen Kato, listed as Tokyo's oldest man, for his 111th birthday found his remains.
He had probably died around 1978, and his family had kept collecting his pension. Checks across Japan then turned up
hundreds of centenarians unaccounted for. The "8050 problem" is parents in their eighties still supporting a withdrawn
child in their fifties, and what becomes of the child when the parent dies (now often called 9060).
- **Why it fits:** an apartment where a family keeps something to itself, and a parent who protects a grown child at
  any cost.
- **What we take:** Haruki's mother kept a secret for him (the abstract's truth), and the apartment is still hers: her
  slippers, her room, her altar, her diary.

### 2.2 Winter bath deaths ("heat shock")
Thousands of older people die in their baths in Japan each winter. Government statistics counted 6,909 bathtub
drownings in 2023, and estimates of all bath-related sudden deaths run up to about 19,000 a year. The sudden change of
temperature, from a cold changing room into a hot bath, is thought to cause most of them.
- **What we take:** Haruki's mother died in the bath last winter, and he showers now. Running the bath for his guest
  (a Japanese host's courtesy: the guest bathes first) is the hardest small task in the game.

### 2.3 The woman in the closet (Kasuya, Fukuoka, 2008)
A man noticed food disappearing from his kitchen and set up a camera that sent pictures to his phone, and it showed
someone moving about his empty house. Police found a 58-year-old woman living on the top shelf of his bedroom closet;
she had been there about a year.
- **Why it fits:** the *oshiire* (the deep sliding-door closet of a Japanese room) is the perfect place for something
  you didn't know was in your home.
- **What we take:** the oshiire in the guest room, the futon kept in it, knocking from inside it at night. And, for
  the player who thinks it through, a visitor who has been in town four days longer than she says.

### 2.4 Hikikomori
The Cabinet Office's 2023 survey estimated about 1.46 million people aged 15 to 64, roughly 2%, living in social
withdrawal.
- **What we take:** Haruki has gone no further than the convenience store since his mother died. Tonight is the first
  time in months anyone has come inside.

### 2.5 Stigmatized property (*jiko bukken*)
Since October 2021, an MLIT guideline tells brokers when a death in a property must be disclosed: a natural death
usually needn't be, unless it was discovered late and needed special cleaning. A website (Ōshimaland) maps such
properties. Chilla's Art already made a game of it (*Stigmatized Property*, 2019), so for us it stays a line in the
mother's story, not the premise.

## 3. Someone who isn't who they say

### 3.1 The Imposter (1997; the documentary, 2012)
Frédéric Bourdin, a 23-year-old French con man, presented himself as Nicholas Barclay, a Texan boy missing for three
years. The family took him in although he was seven years older, had a French accent and had brown eyes where
Nicholas had blue. One account has it that the family wanted to believe: the sister accepted him first, and the rest
followed.
- **Why it fits:** grief makes people see who they want to see. That's Haruki opening his door to "Natsumi".

### 3.2 Rental families
In Tokyo, Yuichi Ishii's agency Family Romance rents out people to stand in as a husband, a father or a friend. Werner
Herzog's *Family Romance, LLC* (2019) follows Ishii as a hired father, and the 2025 film *Rental Family* was drawn
from the same industry.
- **What we take:** a theory the player can have mid-game ("is she hired?"), and a tell to go with it: she knows the
  part well but not the private lines.

### 3.3 A sibling who won't stop looking (Zama, 2017: the shape only)
The 2017 Zama murders came to light after the brother of a missing 23-year-old traced her social media to the man she
had been meeting. It was a real crime with real victims. We take one shape from it and nothing else: when the police
and the family stop, a sibling keeps looking. No names, no details and nothing of the crime goes into the game.

## 4. The dead who come to visit

### 4.1 *Tōno Monogatari* (1910), tale 8: the woman of Samuto
In Kunio Yanagita's collection of folk tales from Tōno, Iwate, a girl of Samuto vanishes, spirited away
(*kamikakushi*), leaving her straw sandals under a pear tree. Decades later she comes back, old, to her family's house
on a day of hard wind, then leaves again. People in Tōno still say on windy days that the Samuto woman might come home.
(The search reached a scholarly summary, not the text itself: check the wording in a translation before quoting it.)
- **What we take:** the oldest shape of our story, from the same corner of Japan: someone lost comes back once, on a
  day of bad weather, and isn't quite who they were.

### 4.2 The ghost passengers of Ishinomaki
For her graduation thesis, Yuka Kudo, a sociology student at Tohoku Gakuin University, interviewed taxi drivers in
Ishinomaki after the 2011 tsunami (the Asahi Shimbun reported it in 2016). Seven described passengers who asked to go
to places the wave had erased and were gone on arrival. The meters had been running, so the drivers recorded the
fares and paid them themselves. They weren't frightened, and some treasured the experience.
- **What we take:** the dead who come back aren't angry; they want to be taken home. That's Natsumi in the secret
  ending.

### 4.3 *Ghosts of the Tsunami* (Richard Lloyd Parry, 2017)
Parry's book centres on Ōkawa Primary School, where 74 children died, and on the Reverend Taio Kaneda, a Zen priest in
Kurihara. Within a fortnight of the disaster he was called to people possessed by the dead, and he went on performing
exorcisms for months.
- **What we take:** possession as something people in modern Tohoku report, and a priest who neither mocks nor fears
  it. We borrow the idea that grief can visit, never the disaster itself (§9).

### 4.4 Itako: the dead speaking through a medium
The *itako* of Aomori, traditionally blind women, are best known for *kuchiyose*, letting the dead speak through them.
The Osorezan summer festival made them nationally famous, and the Meiji government tried to ban them in 1873.
- **What we take:** a line of hers ("Grandma said the dead borrow our mouths"), and a possibility for the player to
  weigh: that the visitor is speaking for someone else.

## 5. New Year's Eve: the night visitors come

### 5.1 Ōsōji and the year god
At the end of December, households do the big clean (*ōsōji*) to purify the home and welcome *Toshigami-sama*, the god
of the new year.
- **What we take:** the opening's hidden timed clean-up is the ōsōji. You clean for a visitor, as every house in
  Japan does tonight. In the bad ending the apartment is spotless again by morning.

### 5.2 Toshikoshi soba
"Year-crossing" soba on New Year's Eve, an Edo-period custom: long noodles for a long life, and noodles that snap
easily to cut away the year's misfortune.
- **What we take:** the dinner request: boil the soba, cut the negi, serve it before midnight.

### 5.3 Joya no kane: the 108 bells
On New Year's Eve, temples ring their bells 108 times, one for each of the 108 earthly desires in Buddhist teaching.
Usually 107 strokes come before midnight and the last one after it, starting around eleven.
- **What we take:** the bells across the river as the night's clock, the midnight moment, and a secret: a player who
  counts hears a 109th.

### 5.4 Raihōshin: deities who visit in masks (UNESCO, 2018)
On 29 November 2018, UNESCO inscribed ten rituals from eight prefectures as *Raiho-shin, ritual visits of deities in
masks and costumes*. Villagers dressed as gods from another world visit houses at the new year to bring luck and scold
laziness. The best known are the Namahage of Oga, Akita, who now come on 31 December. *Raihōshin* (来訪神) shares its
来訪, "a visit", with our Japanese title, 来訪者.
- **What we take:** the title's Japanese, a TV item on the Namahage during the evening, and the idea under the whole
  game: on this night a visitor comes to judge how you keep your house.

### 5.5 Marebito
Origuchi Shinobu's word (1929) for a visiting god from the other world across the sea (*tokoyo*), feared and welcomed
at once, who brings blessings at set times of year. In time, strangers and travellers came to be treated the same way.
- **What we take:** the visitor as someone you must be a good host to even while you're afraid of her.

### 5.6 Somin Shōrai: a god who tests your hospitality
A travelling god asks two brothers for lodging. The rich one refuses and the poor one takes him in, and the god spares
the poor brother's family from plague. The story comes from an 8th-century gazetteer of Bingo province, and charms
naming "the descendants of Somin Shōrai" are still handed out at shrines.
- **What we take:** every request in the game (tea, soba, the bath, the futon) is the host's test, and the endings
  remember how you did.

### 5.7 2026 is a Fire Horse year (*hinoeuma*)
In folk belief, a woman born in a Fire Horse year is headstrong and ruins her husband. In 1966, the last one, Japan's
births fell by roughly a fifth (sources give 15% to 25%). 2026 is the next one, and the game happens on its last
night. Natsumi vanished at the end of 2014, also a horse year: twelve years, the zodiac come full circle.
- **What we take:** an old superstition that blames women, on the night a man's fear turns a woman into his monster.
  One line on the TV, one from her.

## 5b. Summer instead: Obon (the user's change, DECISIONS 2026-10-28, night)

The user moved the game to a summer day, the anniversary of her disappearance. §5 stays as background (the visiting
gods and the hospitality test still frame the visitor); what the summer gives:

### 5b.1 Obon: the dead come home
Mid-August (13 to 16 in most of Japan), the dead return to their families. A welcoming fire (*mukaebi*) is lit at the
door on the 13th and a sending-off fire (*okuribi*) on the 16th; the altar gets a cucumber horse, so the dead come
quickly, and an eggplant cow, so they go back slowly (*shōryō-uma*). The first Obon after a death (*hatsubon*,
*niibon*) is kept with special care, a white lantern among its signs. On the last night, paper lanterns with candles
are floated down rivers to the sea (*tōrō nagashi*).
- **What we take:** the day itself, 16 August: the dead are here until tonight's fire. It's his mother's first Obon,
  and, since her funeral this spring, Natsumi's. Two cucumber horses on his mother's altar (he made one for Natsumi).
  The lanterns on the river from the balcony, and one floating upstream. The ending that lights the fire nobody lit.

### 5b.2 Summer rivers
Water accidents peak in July and August; the National Police Agency counted 436 water-related incidents in summer
2026, and the Nippon Foundation's analysis of summer 2025 found most drowning victims died. Evening storms (*yūdachi*)
swell rivers fast.
- **What we take:** the weir below the bridge, the river high after the afternoon storm, and a storm tonight too.

### 5b.3 The broken geta strap
A snapped *hanao*, the thong of a wooden sandal, is a well-known bad omen, like a broken comb.
- **What we take:** Natsumi's strap snaps on the walk home; she leaves the geta on the bank (Tōno's sandals under the
  pear tree, §4.1) and runs barefoot onto the weir. He keeps the geta.

### 5b.4 Coloured contact lenses
Coloured contacts are a fashion staple for young Japanese women; since November 2009 even non-corrective ones are
medical devices needing the health ministry's approval, after reports of eye damage, and the approved products went
from under ten in 2009 to about 300 by 2013.
- **What we take:** Natsumi's rare lilac eyes, and Fuyumi's lilac lenses: nearly right, too even, one lost in the
  bath.

### 5b.5 A child can't be charged
Japan's Penal Code, art. 41: an act of a person under 14 is not punishable; a child under the age goes to a child
guidance centre, not a court.
- **What we take:** Haruki and Natsumi were thirteen, so the intended ending can't be a confession to the police.
  What his silence owes is owed to her family.

## 6. Folk tells we can use

### 6.1 "Moshi moshi" and the single call
The Japanese answer the phone with "moshi moshi", said twice. A popular story, spread in English since about 2009,
says foxes and ghosts can't say it twice, so saying it proves you're human. The dictionary origin is *mōshi* ("I say")
repeated, and the doubling may simply come from bad early phone lines. Older is the mountain *yōkai* called
*hitokoe-yobi*, "the one-call": it calls your name once, and you mustn't answer.
- **What we take:** she phones from the station and says "moshi moshi" twice (she's human, as anyone who knows the
  story will note). After midnight the phone rings, and a girl's voice says "moshi" once.

### 6.2 Salt at the door (*kiyome no shio*)
After a funeral, mourners sprinkle purifying salt on their chest, back and feet before going into their homes, so the
impurity of death stays outside. Funeral homes hand out the packets.
- **What we take:** a packet from his mother's funeral sits in the genkan drawer. A player who thinks she's dead can
  salt her at the door. She laughs, hurt: "Did someone die?"

### 6.3 Kagome Kagome
A children's game: one child sits blindfolded in the middle and the others circle, singing. When the song stops, the
child in the middle must name who is right behind them (*ushiro no shōmen dāre?*). The traditional words vary by
region and are famously cryptic (a bird in a cage that comes out "on the night of dawn", "the crane and the turtle
slipped"); nobody knows what they first meant.
- **What we take:** the game played with her in the apartment, her hands over your eyes and your guess to make. The
  song's last question is the game's own (who is she, really?), and "slipped" is what happened on the ice.

### 6.4 The prohibition of looking
The crane wife who forbids her husband to watch her weave (*Tsuru no Ongaeshi*), Izanagi looking at Izanami in the
underworld, the snow woman (*Yuki-onna*) who must never be spoken of: the psychoanalyst Osamu Kitayama called this
motif "the prohibition of looking" (*miru na no kinshi*). Break it and you lose the one you love.
- **What we take:** "Don't listen, okay?" when she phones her mother, and "Don't come in" at the bath. The player can
  break either, and learns something.

### 6.5 Fox possession (*kitsunetsuki*)
For centuries Japan explained madness as possession by a fox. Meiji-era psychiatry tried to reclassify it as illness,
with limited success, and it stayed a common explanation into the 20th century (the possessed were said to crave
fried tofu and avoid your eyes).
- **What we take:** Haruki's illness reaches for the old explanation, "something is wearing her face", and the player
  is invited to believe it.

## 7. The mind

### 7.1 Capgras
The Capgras delusion is the conviction that someone close has been replaced by an identical impostor. It occurs in
psychiatric illness, schizophrenia among them, and more often in neurological conditions. The leading account: the
face is recognised but the feeling of familiarity is gone (patients show no skin-conductance response to familiar
faces).
- **Why it fits:** "Did she always look like this?" (the user's own line) is the Capgras question. Capgras is one of
  the *delusional misidentification* syndromes; in the revised abstract Haruki's is the other way round (he sees the
  sister as the girl he lost), which is closer to that family's Frégoli pattern, where someone is taken for a familiar
  person. We show it, never name it.

### 7.2 Paranoia reads anger into neutral faces
People with schizophrenia have a large deficit in recognising emotion in faces, worst for negative emotions (Kohler et
al.'s meta-analysis, *Schizophrenia Bulletin*, 2010). And actively paranoid patients are more likely than others to
see a neutral face as angry (Pinkham, Brensinger, Kohler, Gur and Gur, 2011).
- **Why it fits:** this is the mechanic. L.A. Noire asks "is she lying?" and trusts the face to show it. Our
  narrator's reading of her face is bent by paranoia: the inner monologue says she's smirking while the performance
  shows a frightened woman.

### 7.3 Schizophrenia in Japan
In 2002 Japan renamed schizophrenia from *seishin bunretsu byō* ("mind-split disease") to *tōgō shitchō shō*
("integration disorder"), after a request from a national federation of patients' families in 1993. Studies since
find stigma somewhat reduced, while the news still ties the illness to crime. The history behind it is hard: under the
1900 custody law, families confined mentally ill relatives at home (*shitaku kanchi*), and Shūzō Kure's 1918 report on
364 such rooms said these patients suffered twice, from the illness and from being born in this country.
- **What we take:** the term on his discharge papers, and the weight of a family hiding illness at home (his mother
  did, in her own way).

### 7.4 The props of treatment
Every patient in Japan has an *okusuri techō*, a medication notebook that the pharmacy adds a sticker to with each
prescription. Medicine comes in printed pharmacy bags carrying the patient's name and when to take it.
- **What we take:** the bathroom cabinet: a pharmacy bag in Haruki's name (an antipsychotic by its generic name, "once
  a day, after dinner"), the notebook with its last sticker on 28 November, and a pill organiser with two weeks
  untaken.

### 7.5 Care: the facts about violence
People with schizophrenia are far more often victims of violence than perpetrators, and patients in treatment are no
more violent than anyone else. Even so, news stories present them as perpetrators many times more often than as
victims.
- **How we write it:** Haruki isn't a monster with a diagnosis. What he did twelve years ago he did at eighteen, before
  he was ill, and what hides it from him is grief, a lie he was given and the illness that grew round them. The bad
  ending is the tragedy of a man untreated and alone making a choice, never a "gotcha"; the other two endings show him
  helped. The game opens with a content note and ends with a short resources screen.

### 7.6 Illness read as a haunting: Elisa Lam (2013)
A student was found in the rooftop water tank of a Los Angeles hotel. The lift footage from her last hours spread
online as evidence of a haunting. The coroner ruled the death an accidental drowning, with her bipolar disorder a
significant factor.
- **What we take:** how readily people read illness as the supernatural, and the facts they skip. Our game lets the
  player do the same, then shows them.

## 9. What we take, and what we don't

- **Shapes, not material.** Real cases give us how things happen (a sibling keeps looking, a family accepts an
  impostor, a mother lies for her son), never names, victims or details.
- **Grief, not disaster.** The tsunami's dead taught us that the dead who visit want to go home. The game's death is a
  private one, a girl through river ice in a fictional town.
- **Folklore as the characters' frame.** The visiting gods, the fox and the single call are how Haruki (and the
  player) try to explain her. The game never needs them to be true, except in its secret ending.
- **Illness with care** (§7.5).

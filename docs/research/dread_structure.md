# How short horror games build dread: the rules, the beats, a curve for our games

2026-11-05. The user: "I want my games to be simple but build up to a story / uncovery / dread." Three notes answer
it: this one (how dread is built over a running time), research/story_in_objects.md (how a story is uncovered through
things) and research/simple_loops.md (one loop, a night or a shift, escalating). Read as search extracts: the session's
network reaches GitHub and little else, so every quote is a search engine's extract of the page named, not the page
(about 45 searches for this note). Inferences are marked as mine.

## 1. What the designers say

- **Thomas Grip, Frictional (Amnesia, SOMA).** The game as "a sensory deprivation chamber": "focus on evoking emotion";
  darkness "should be an enemy"; the team found it could "trick the players into scaring themselves"; the sanity meter
  had "no real failed state" and "players imagined the consequences themselves" (GDC Europe 2011). The *4 layers* (2014,
  with Chmielarz): gameplay; a narrative goal ("since the goal is narrative in nature, it becomes a reward for completing
  the gameplay section"); narrative background (the player "doing stuff in order to make the story appear"); mental
  modelling (the systems "only really become evident for the player if they do something wrong or when they directly
  contradict their mental model"). The *SSM framework* (2017): System, Story, Mental Model (not "sensation"): players
  "must constantly fill in blanks in our knowledge to properly create a mental image of the world". *9 years, 9 lessons*
  (2019): "horror isn't enjoyable" as entertainment's paradox; "scares alone aren't enough ... less is often more";
  "narrative is core". A confessed mistake: in Amnesia "the placement of diary entries and notes made no real sense";
  Rebirth "had the game story unfold as the player progresses".
- **Chris Pruett (Chris's Survival Horror Quest; The Mechanics of Tension, 2015).** "Tension is an emotion rooted in
  delay"; "the primer hooks the audience early, sets up the engine for tension"; "tension comes from the impending
  scare if the player fails"; "robbing the player of predictability is what makes the game unsettling"; "waves and
  rhythms create stress relief for players"; sound is "about 80 % of the effectiveness of Slender"; a jump scare "may
  only irritate the player if there is an improper lead-up ... due to lack of tension, music, or atmosphere". His
  faults: Silent Hill 4 has "no predictable rise and fall of tension"; in Outlast "item placement and pop-out scare
  timing are easy to anticipate well in advance". His list of means: protagonist vulnerability, sound, unpredictable
  systems, rule obfuscation, meaningful failure, perception hacks, limiting control, raising the heart rate.
- **Capcom.** Mikami: for RE1 to 3 "scaring the player is the number one thing"; RE4 put fun first (the RE4 tension
  graph, the "feel-good hump", was not found in any source). RE7 (GDC 2017): "a heightened level of fear paired with a
  chance for players to fight back"; the opening's hand-chop was added because the beginning "lacked punch"; "harder
  to feel fear or horror in a game of that scale". RE2's remake: the original's fear came from "fixed camera angles
  hiding what was nearby".
- **Supermassive, Until Dawn (the five-step formula; Will Byles).** No "steady stream" of jump scares, "since players
  become desensitized"; scares "telegraphed in some cases and obscured in others, or players learn the formula";
  tension is "the quiet dread of not knowing what happens next, with long stretches of nothing after a suggested
  scare"; "a scare fades each time a player sees it" (measured on fresh players' skin); "deliberately languid pacing
  initially proved contentious".
- **The build and release essays (Game Developer, Game Wisdom).** "Each build-up has to be followed by a release";
  Dead Space "attacks the player so often that tension never accumulates"; story-horror "raising tension without ever
  releasing it"; the ideal player "oscillates between being frightened and being on edge". *The back half problem*:
  "most horror games run out of steam before the end"; "every horror game always starts the player the same way: weak
  and helpless"; "horror design isn't really built for continued escalation", so the back half needs new elements,
  not louder ones. "Starting slowly and building gradually ... keeps the player on edge by defying what players expect
  from horror."
- **FAITH (Mason Smith, GDC 2021).** Scares engineered to say "the danger is growing worse"; a long flashback cut
  "because it messed up the pacing".
- **Puppet Combo.** "Horror, and slashers especially, benefit from a shorter runtime"; games "that can be beaten in a
  few sittings".
- **Chilla's Art.** Their one design statement: "there's something eerie about Japanese scenery, such as public
  telephones, tunnels, and subway platforms". The critics supply the rest: "minimal use of jump scares"; "the greatest
  suspense the developers build occurs when they erase all background noise completely"; "the naive faith that our
  routines are inviolable [is] what makes the games unsettling"; "ordinary jobs and ordinary places gone wrong"; games
  "typically span the length of said night"; "most have two endings".
- **Anatomy (Kitty Horrorshow).** "Listen to a tape, go to the next room it tells you"; "the house continues to degrade
  more and more upon continued playing"; fear built "incrementally enough that the escalation never becomes corny or
  crass"; the house reads "abandoned, then lonely, then bitter".
- **P.T.** "Each time a loop is successfully completed, changes appear in the corridor"; "at its literal and structural
  centre is the corner ... if the corridor hasn't changed, then something else has"; the "repeating, rhythmically
  iterating structure focuses the senses on each and every change"; late loops: "Look behind you" on the radio, red
  lamps, an endless corridor.
- **Silent Hill 2.** "Foggy world, darker world, back and forth, reality breaks down, end"; "timing is a single
  unanticipated thump; pacing is a long walk in the fog, during which nothing happens at all".
- **The Mortuary Assistant.** The developer's term, the "dread scare": the game "pushes you toward something a
  genre-savvy player knows is dangerous".

**Numbers.** No designer gives any. A sound-design blog (low authority): stingers "no more than one every 60 to 90
seconds of play, ideally less"; "the scariest part of a horror game is usually the five minutes when nothing is
happening"; "sustain maximum intensity for ten minutes and the player adapts"; "fake out the player more often than
they actually scare them".

**J-horror as a structure.** Ringu: the curse "is activated by an action, and ... the rules are discoverable"; "minimal
score, held takes, horror in the background of a family drama"; the seven days and the call are "the mechanism".
Ju-On: "every character is condemned the moment they appear". The contrast: "one is about inescapable fate; the other
is about knowable, if terrible, rules". Kurosawa (Pulse) holds "the camera on an apparently empty background so the
viewer scans for a focal point, and the ghost looks like an ordinary woman"; people "slowly disappear ... in quiet,
almost resigned dissolutions". Ito: Uzumaki "starts small but continues to expand", "constant building and release of
intense dread", no resolution; The Enigma of Amigara Fault "does not introduce us to any external dangers", the horror
"entirely at the end", never shown.

**Simplicity.** A game "frightens because nothing is happening on screen"; "too much power removes fear, and too
little makes hiding routine"; The Mortuary Assistant's order and repetition "that the uncanny then invades", with the
risk that "tasks can feel repetitive after several shifts". No designer's statement on limited verbs was found; the
extracts above imply it (mine).

## 3. A dread curve for 150 to 180 minutes (mine, from the numbers above)

The unit is the **shift** (Chilla's night, the Mortuary's shift, the Hotline's day, P.T.'s loop): our format's night as
a chapter. Chilla's 40-minute games spend three nights and a finale; the Hotline seven days; the Mortuary a boring
first hour; Until Dawn "long stretches of nothing after a suggested scare"; ten minutes of intensity and the player
adapts. Scaled to 150 minutes: **four shifts of about 30 minutes and a finale of 20 to 30**, each shift a build and a
release ending somewhere safe (Yomawari's home, Chilla's going home). simple_loops.md works the same budget out as six
nights of 25 minutes; either fits.

- **By minute 10 (shift 1 begins).** The routine taught by doing it (a narrative goal per shift: Grip's layer 2). One
  deniable wrongness within the first ten minutes: a camera, a sound, a thing moved (the Store's boy on the monitor;
  Pruett's primer). No threat, no failure state. Silence as the instrument (Chilla's erased room tone).
- **By minute 30 (shift 1 ends).** Nothing has happened; the player has a model of the place. One "if the corridor
  hasn't changed, then something else has" moment when they turn back. Release: the shift ends.
- **By minute 60 (shift 2).** The first sighting: brief, ordinary-looking, at the edge (Kurosawa; Yomawari's
  periphery), and the first rule learned (Ringu: the thing has a mechanism). The first story fragment (letter 1 of 3).
  The routine still holds; the game fakes out more than it scares.
- **By minute 90 (shift 3).** The routine breaks (the Closing Shift's extra shift, the Hotline losing its database, the
  Mortuary's breaker): the first real threat with a failure state, telegraphed (Until Dawn: telegraph some, obscure
  others). Fragment 2. Let players scare themselves; one chase at most. Then a true quiet of five minutes.
- **By minute 120 (shift 4, the turn).** The place is wrong at its root (Devotion's rooms as years, Anatomy's house
  speaking, P.T.'s red loop): a new element, not a louder old one (the back half problem); the mental model directly
  contradicted (layer 4). The one obscured scare of the game.
- **By minute 150 (the finale).** The last fragment tells the house's story (the tape, letter 3); the choice (watch it
  or box it; call for help or not); the chase or the walk; two endings (Chilla's norm), the better one bittersweet.
- **Rhythm.** Build 10 to 15 minutes, release 3 to 5; a scare at most every 20 to 30 minutes in the first hour, closer
  later; stingers no more than one a minute even in the finale; never the same scare twice (it fades each time).

## 4. What it means for our tools

- **The writers' room's chart** already has the shapes (scene, talk, task, chapter, choice, secret, ending): a game's
  chart could be read for its curve: how many minutes of tasks before the first scare beat, scares per chapter, whether
  a release (a safe scene, a bed) follows each build. A `/chart` note could count them (the story check's walker has the
  times: `storylib`'s clip lengths and the tasks' `timer`s). Mine; not built.
- **The format's scares** are words already (`knock`, `whisper`, `lights_die`, `glimpse`, `swap`, `jumpscare`, the
  flashlight's `flicker`, the look-away `swap`); the curve says which goes where: `glimpse` and `swap` in shifts 1 and
  2 (the deniable), `knock` and `lights_die` in 3 (the telegraphed), `jumpscare` once, in 4 (the obscured).
- **The little things** are the deniable wrongness with no scare word at all: a kettle that boiled by itself, a TV
  turned to static (`state`), a clock stopped (TestRoom001's `n_sleep`): Anatomy's house and P.T.'s corridor are made of
  these. The to-do list sends the player to them, which is what makes the change seen (simple_loops.md §2: 4.2 % notice
  a gradual change unaided).
- **Nights as chapters** (`sleep`, `chapter`, the autosave) are the release the essays ask for; the plan in
  simple_loops.md §3 fills each night.
- **Text, not voice** (DECISIONS 2026-10-30) suits Chilla's silence: the room tone erased is the cheapest scare we have
  (`music` off, the shelf's room tones out).

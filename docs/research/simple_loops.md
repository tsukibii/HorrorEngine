# One loop that escalates: nights, shifts and the house that changes

2026-11-05. The third note for the user's "simple but build up to a story / uncovery / dread" (with
research/dread_structure.md and research/story_in_objects.md): games built on one simple loop, a night, a shift, a
day of chores, that escalates into story and dread, and the numbers to plan ours by. Read as search extracts (40);
every quote is an extract's wording. Inferences are marked as mine.

## 2. What the designers say

- **Lucas Pope (Papers, Please).** "A rigid bureaucracy works really well with how I like to design game mechanics";
  the family at night was "intended to add pressure to your daily processing"; the rules "grow more complicated over
  time and are not just there to make the game harder".
- **Scott Cawthon (FNaF).** The monitor "because limited visibility and mobility were instinctively terrifying"; "the
  provision of a false sense of security to players as a fear tactic". Nothing on why five nights.
- **Brian Clarke, DarkStone (The Mortuary Assistant).** The Haunt System above; his analogy, "a presumptuous date who
  insists on ordering for you". The reviews' counter: "you'll start to see the exact same scares by your second shift
  making them rather tedious". Randomised scares buy a first run, not a second (mine).
- **Chilla's Art.** They "generate many ideas once they settle on making a short-form Japanese horror game" and aim at
  Let's Play coverage; the critics: games "almost always built around everyday tasks", the night shift "the base for
  slow-burn horror", "the mundane setting makes the scares land harder", "understanding restraint, with scares timed
  to catch players at the worst moment".
- **Nick Lives (Home Safety Hotline).** "All calls have a consequence that occurs if you give them the wrong info";
  "not all entries are used, indeed many will be red herrings". The catalogue the player reads is the story's drip,
  and the last day quizzes them on their own log.
- **Black Salt (Dredge).** "The tension comes from the unknown, and the team holds a lot back and lets players imagine
  things"; "daytime doesn't build up insanity because you can see everything".
- **Witch Beam (Unpacking).** The "stealth storyline": "absorb the life of the person you're unpacking for without
  necessarily realising that you're being told a story"; no branching because paths introduce "FOMO, choice judgment,
  and unwanted outcomes".
- **Time loops (Outer Wilds).** "The only thing you can bring back in time with you is your memories"; the team asked
  "are players ready to learn? Will they care about this information?"
- **Tension and release.** "A game with only buildup never gives the player relief"; "long stretches of dread without
  a payoff leave players calm and unafraid"; "repeated encounters lead players to treat a game as a checklist of fixed
  scares, so varying enemy placement and events helps"; "horror mechanics are ordinary and only gain dread from
  context" (Jared Mitchell).
- **The house that changes.** P.T.: "repetition is usually anathema for horror ... P.T.'s strength is the small
  variations between loops"; "some changes are so subtle players may miss them entirely". Layers of Fear: "turning
  around shows a wall where a corridor had just been"; the team "used lighting or sounds to entice the player into
  triggering those effects"; the critique: "similar tricks ad nauseam". **The one number:** in a room-escape study
  (Marples et al., 2019) "only a very small proportion of participants (4.20 %) detected gradual textural changes to the
  environment across durations from 90 seconds down to 11.25 seconds. Duration made no significant difference"; the
  gorilla study: half of all observers missed the unexpected event, 64 % noticed it during an easy task and 45 % during
  a hard one. So (mine): a moved thing is noticed only if the task sends the player to it, or it is a hard cut (a wall
  where a door was), or it is something the little things make the player watch (the kettle, the clock, the TV).

## 3. A night-by-night plan for 2.5 to 3 hours (mine, from the numbers)

**The numbers.** Units in the genre: 4 (Stories Untold, Anatomy), 5 (FNaF, Fears to Fathom), about 7 (the Hotline), 8
(Unpacking), 14 (No, I'm Not a Human), 16 and an epilogue (Strange Horticulture), 31 (Papers, Please). Unit lengths:
FNaF 9 minutes (one room, no chores); Chilla's 40 to 100 minutes a whole game; the Hotline about 25 minutes a day; a
Mortuary shift of 90 to 120 minutes is a whole game in one. Scare scheduling: NIANH's threat on 3, 5, 9, 11 (every other
night, then gaps), the authority taking people on 4 and 5 and 8 and 10, two empty nights (6 and 13) right before the
end. The break: FNaF's night 5 (the voice replaced) and 6 (no voice); the Mortuary's last ending; Anatomy's fourth run;
the Hotline's trial built from the player's own log.

**Budget.** Six nights of about 25 minutes make 150, plus title cards and an epilogue of 10 to 15: about 2 h 40. Six is
the count where every night adds exactly one new thing and the fifth can break (dread_structure.md's curve has four
shifts of 30 and a finale: the same budget cut coarser). A seventh only as a FNaF "night 6": after the ending, hard, no
story.

| Night | Adds (one each: a task, a rule, a wrongness, a story piece, a scare) | Model |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Arrival** | the loop taught by the to-do list (four or five chores: the kettle, the locks, the feeding, bed); the little things introduced; one rule from her ("don't go upstairs"); the story: who you are, who's in the house; **no scare**, one unexplained sound; bed, a title card, the autosave | FNaF's night 1, every AI at 0; Papers' day 1; the Hotline's frozen pipes and mice |
| **2. The first rule** | one new chore and one new rule ("the phone: don't answer after ten"); the phone rings anyway (the drip, as the phone calls open FNaF's nights); **one thing moved**, pointed at by a chore (4.2 % notice a gradual change unaided); a scare: a knock, unanswered | Papers' new rules a day; the Hotline's consequence for a wrong answer |
| **3. First contact** | a chore that can fail (the pot boils over, the stain); a rule contradicted mid-night (she says the opposite of night 1: the D.D.D.'s "instructions that contradict earlier guidance"); the first direct scare at the night's end, then bed | NIANH's Pale Man on night 3; FNaF's night 3 |
| **4. The turn, then quiet** | the story's reveal through the little things (the TV's static clears for one broadcast; a letter); **something taken away** (a character, a room locked, a thing gone); the second half of the night empty: the release | FNaF's night 4 (the caller dies); NIANH's FEMA "removes one person"; the no-guest nights; the essays' release |
| **5. The night that isn't a night** | the chores come back but the house refuses them (the kettle won't boil, the door won't lock); the to-do list is written by someone else; the voice that taught you is gone or wrong; the bed doesn't end the night; the one chase or grab; **no autosave at the bed, a checkpoint elsewhere** | FNaF's night 5 (the distorted call); Anatomy "peeling away the rules of the last"; the Hotline's riddles from your own log; the Mortuary's final ending |
| **6. Epilogue** | outside the loop: daylight, one room, one task; the ending by the kept rules, not a last choice; 10 to 15 minutes | Strange Horticulture's epilogue; Stories Untold's fourth episode reusing the three; Papers' endings "not on a single decision" |

**Rules to keep (mine).** One new element a night, never two. Every rule is a chore the player does with their hands
(mechanics "only gain dread from context"). A change the player must notice is on the to-do list's path. The voice
that explains (the phone, the notes, her) thins night by night and goes on the break. The quiet night comes right
before the break, not after. Randomise where a scare fires within a night, never which night (the Mortuary's lesson:
randomness reads as repetition by the second shift). The wrongness escalates in kind, not in size: a sound (1), a
thing (2), a person (3), the house's rules (4), the loop itself (5).

## 4. What it means for our tools

- **Nights as chapters** are the unit already: `sleep` in a bed with an `if` (the chores first), a `chapter` card, the
  autosave, the time of day (`flowTimes`); night 5's "the bed doesn't end the night" is a `sleep` whose `if` never holds
  and a `checkpoint` elsewhere.
- **The to-do list** (`todo`, `tick`, `fail`, a request with `who`) is the loop's teacher and the change's pointer; a
  list "written by someone else" is a `todo` set by a beat with another voice's words (the game's words by what's
  happened, STORY_FORMAT §9.14).
- **The little things** carry the wrongness without a scare word: `state` on the kettle, the TV, the clock, the phone;
  night 5's refusals are `acts: false` and a `secs` that never ends (mine: a life state that holds would want a word).
- **The rules** are her lines (`say`) and the Database's words; a contradiction is the same line's second version
  picked by a flag (§9.14 again).
- **The Director** keeps a quest at a time with its steps, which is a night; its easter eggs are the red herrings; its
  states (who is where, what's locked, what's lit) are "something taken away".
- **The writers' room's chart** could carry a night as an act (a frame) with its five slots named in the inspector (a
  task, a rule, a wrongness, a piece, a scare), so a chart that misses one shows it; `/chart` could lay a six-night
  skeleton out from a note. Mine; not built.
